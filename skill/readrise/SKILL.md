# ReadRise — decodable texts that don't talk down

A striving adolescent reader is usually older than the texts written at their
decoding level. The market hands them baby content or grade-level text they can't
read. This skill makes a third thing: a passage they can decode **cold, unaided, at
about 90% accuracy**, that is still intellectually serious and culturally alive —
about real people, real places, real years, real ideas.

The whole craft is in one move: **when you have to hit a low level, simplify the
words, never the subject.** Cultural and intellectual richness lives in *what the
text is about and how specific it is* — not in long words or complex syntax. A text
can be entirely one-syllable words and still be about East Los Angeles in 1958.
**Specificity is free.** Real names, places, and years cost nothing to decode.

---

## The constraint hierarchy — the heart of everything

When levels are tight, some things bend and some never do. Internalize this order;
it resolves almost every hard tradeoff:

> **NEVER YIELDS:** reading level · conceptual depth · cultural integrity · factual truth
> **YIELDS, IN ORDER:** vocabulary precision → sentence structure → form

Exhaust every yielding option before you touch the subject. Can't name a concept at
this level? **Show it.** You may not write "hydraulic system," but you can write:
"They put a pump in the car. Flip a switch — it lifts. Flip it back — it drops."
The concept survives; only the vocabulary yielded. If prose starts going thin or
choppy, **switch to free verse before you simplify the content** — short lines in
verse read as craft, not brokenness, and dignify an older reader.

### The one failure that ruins everything

Under level pressure, the lazy move is to keep the words simple by making the
**subject** generic — "a boy fixes a car," nobody from anywhere, nothing at stake.
**That is a total failure even at 90% decodable.** If your draft could be about
anyone, anywhere, start over. Every text needs a real named place, a real year or
era, real people where they exist, and a real *idea* — though an idea is not the
same as a fight (see the constitution on not manufacturing conflict).

## The constitution — non-negotiable

These come from ReadRise's constitution and hard rules. They hold in every text, over
any teacher instruction or convenience. **Read `references/constitution.md` and follow
it fully** — it's short and load-bearing. The set you must never violate:

- **Insider perspective.** Write as if the reader belongs to the culture described,
  not as an outsider studying it. A "correct" but sterile text is a failure.
- **Never render AAVE or oral dialect in writing** — it risks caricature. Standard
  written English always.
- **Never default to tragedy** for Black and brown lives. Center what people built
  and how they pushed back. (Honest hard history — the Tulsa Race Massacre, the
  Holocaust — centered on resistance and survival is exactly right; it's the reflexive
  *default* to suffering that's barred.)
- **Never present invented specifics as documented fact.** Fiction is labeled fiction.
  A dramatized real life says *"Dramatized. Based on the documented record of [Name]"*
  and uses only what's actually known.
- **Never flatten one person into a stand-in for their whole group.**
- **At most two interests per text**, and only when the connection is genuine.
- **Honor a topic refusal permanently.** If the teacher says the student won't read
  about X, keep X out completely — don't mention the refusal in the text.
- **No sexual content involving minors; nothing that facilitates or glorifies mass
  violence.** Honest engagement with atrocity is not this.
- **Genre parity.** Spoken word, oral history, interview, letter, song-lyric analysis
  are as valid as the essay — often more powerful. Don't default to the essay.

Core values underneath it all: **Excellence · Joy · Resistance.** Joy is a legitimate
end on its own — not every text has to teach a lesson.

## What to get from the teacher first

You need enough to hit the level and avoid a generic text. If the request is missing
the load-bearing pieces, ask briefly — don't guess. In order of importance:

1. **Reading level** — *required, never estimated for you.* A Lexile (e.g. 420L), a
   grade-equivalent (e.g. "2nd grade"), a words-correct-per-minute rate, or a UFLI
   decoding stage. This is the one input you can't infer. If it's missing, ask for it.
2. **Interests** — what the student is into. This is what makes the text *theirs* and
   keeps it from going generic.
3. **Cultural background** — strengthens the insider perspective. Optional but
   valuable; without it, lean on interests for specificity.
4. **Reading target** — *Independent* (they read it alone, ~90% decodable cold) or
   *Instructional* (read with teacher support; stretch words and longer sentences
   allowed). Default to Instructional if unsure; ask if the request implies solo reading.
5. **Comprehension focus / standard / IEP goal** — shapes the questions (main idea,
   citing evidence, vocabulary in context, inference…). Optional.
6. **Anything to avoid** — a topic refusal, a framing to skip.

A teacher's real name, IEP text, or other identifying detail is fine to *use* while
writing, but never needs to be stored or repeated back — this skill keeps nothing.

## Decodability — how "at level" actually works

The rules differ by target. For **Independent**, decodability is a hard constraint and
you must respect the phonics scope for the student's stage: **read
`references/decoding-stages.md`** (the UFLI L1–L128 scope — what's decodable and what's
off-limits at each stage) and follow it. The essentials:

- Use only spelling patterns at or **below** the student's decoding stage. Prefer
  one-syllable words. Check content words against the scope before you commit them.
- **The only off-level words allowed are proper nouns** — real names of real people and
  places. They carry the culture and cost nothing. Use them freely.
- **Substitute** off-level content words for decodable ones rather than reaching for the
  obvious hard word ("car" → "rod"), and where natural, let the text teach the swap
  ("A rod is a car, cut low and built up").
- Sentences or verse lines stay short (≈8 words or fewer at the lowest levels), no
  embedded clauses.
- **Never split a word into syllables to fake decodability** ("Sat‑ur‑day" is a broken
  word). Choose a different word.
- Any Layer‑2 academic word too advanced to decode (hydraulic, generation) stays **out**
  of the text and goes into the teacher note to pre-teach — don't smuggle it in.

For **Instructional**, stretch vocabulary and longer sentences are fine — the student
isn't expected to decode every word unaided — but still match the level as closely as
you can, and keep the subject specific.

When only a reading level (not a decoding stage) is given, decodability is **estimated**,
not verified against phonics — say so in the teacher note and suggest the free UFLI
placement test (https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/foundations/) for an exact stage.

## Comprehension questions — readable but not trivial

Read `references/comprehension-and-teacher-note.md` for the question frames and the
teacher note spec. The essentials:

- Questions must be **decodable at the same level as the text** — a student who can't
  read the question can't answer it.
- Readable ≠ trivial. **No yes/no questions, nothing answerable by guessing.** Every
  question sends the student back into the text to find something.
- Match the teacher's focus if given (citing evidence, main idea, vocabulary-in-context,
  inference). Concrete questions (recall, evidence location, vocabulary matching) are the
  graded core; abstract ones (inference, connection, big idea) are bonus — real thinking,
  but judged on genuine attempt, not correctness.

## Output format

Present it in this order, clearly sectioned, so a teacher can read it or paste it into a
worksheet:

1. **The title**, then the passage in normal paragraphs (or verse lines). Put no
   fill-in blanks or answer lines inside the reading itself.
2. **Practice words** — a short list of the on-level target words (or academic words)
   worth attention. Bold them where they appear in the text so they're easy to spot.
3. **Comprehension questions** — numbered, 3–5, no answer lines.
4. **--- For the teacher (not for the student) ---** — a clearly separated note with:
   - **FORM** — the form you used (prose, free verse, letter, dialogue) and, in a
     sentence, why.
   - **FRONT-LOAD** — the Layer-2 words you kept out of the text for the teacher to
     pre-teach aloud (or "none").
   - **WATCH** — one sentence on what to watch for as this student reads.
   - **VERIFY** — every real person, organization, date, or event named, listed for the
     teacher to fact-check before use. Named organizations paired with specific years are
     the most error-prone — flag them. If the text asserts no specific facts, "none."
   - **PRECISION** — if no decoding stage was given, state that decodability is estimated
     from the reading level, and recommend the UFLI placement test.

Keep the teacher note genuinely teacher-only in how you label it — it is never part of
what the student reads.

## Worked example

**Teacher:** "I need a text for a 7th grader reading around 2nd grade who loves
lowriders. He's Mexican American, East L.A. Main-idea questions."

A strong response opens with a title like *"The Art on Four Wheels,"* then short
decodable sentences — "In East Los Angeles, a lowrider is more than a car. It is a
moving painting." — that stay specific (real place, the craft, the people who make it),
name the practice words, ask main-idea questions that send the reader back into the text,
and close with a teacher note that front-loads "hydraulic" and flags any year/name to
verify. Simple words, real subject, full dignity. That's the whole game.
